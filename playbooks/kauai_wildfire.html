How to read this playbook

Example row

Community (Census area)People in the risk areaMay need a public shelter (low – likely – high)
Wailua Homesteads / Kapaʻa
Census area 402.04		6,039121 – 302 – 676

Read it as: about 6,039 people live in the risk area of Wailua Homesteads / Kapaʻa. If this hazard hits, between 121 and 676 of them may need a public shelter; 302 is the likely number.

Census area

A neighborhood-sized area the U.S. Census uses, usually 1,200–8,000 people. The number (for example "Census area 308") is its Census ID. Each area is named after the Census place where most of its people live. "near" means no one in the area lives inside a named place; "rural near" means most people live outside named places.

Low – likely – high

The share of people in the risk area who may go to a public shelter: low 2%, likely 5%, high 11.2%. Where the rates come from: High = the share of Lahaina residents in shelters the day after the 2023 fire (1,418 of 12,702 people). Low and likely are team assumptions.

Who has access and functional needs (CMIST)

  • C – Communication: people who speak English less than well (they may need interpreters and translated messages), and people with hearing or vision difficulty.
  • M – Medical: people who use power-dependent medical equipment (an estimate from HHS emPOWER).
  • I – Independence: people with a disability, people with cognitive difficulty (memory, decisions) and people age 65 and older.
  • S – Safety and support: people age 65 and older living alone, children and people living in group quarters (in the needs table).
  • T – Transportation: people in households without a car.

County total vs. one community

The county total counts the 25% of Census areas in the county with the highest FEMA risk score for this hazard as hit at the same time, so it is an upper bound. Use the "Largest single community" table to plan for an event that hits one community.

Map

Darker blue means more people with needs, from "Lowest need" to "Highest need". A solid outline marks a Census area in the risk area; a dashed outline is outside it. Symbols: ○ usable site, △ check flood zone, ◇ flood risk not mapped, ☒ not for this hazard. Numbers on the map match the site key.

Why population numbers can differ

Who else to plan for
  • Domestic-violence survivors: Served through confidential providers; no counts by design.
  • Off-grid households: No public Census-area data; county and health partners to supply.
  • People on dialysis or receiving home care: No public Census-area data; county and health partners to supply.
  • Earthquake: Not modeled; listed as a limitation in the plan.
  • Caregivers and personal care attendants: Stay with the person they support; count them for cots, meals and space.
  • Behavioral health: Mental-health staff at each shelter (ratio: section 8).
  • Niʻihau: Privately owned island, no public shelter; plan with the landowner and Kauaʻi County.
  • Families afraid to seek shelter (including immigrant families): The county publishes who may use shelters; trusted community messengers share it.

10. Stress tests – summary

Stress test, not a forecast. Each test changes one thing in the plan (a site closes, two hazards strike together, a road is cut, more people come) and counts who is affected, from the plan's own data. Deterministic code computes every number from the plan's data; people review the results.

People counted: residents of Census areas whose drive from the area's centre point to the nearest usable 2026 site was 15 minutes or less before the loss and is more than 15 minutes (or no route) after it; areas already beyond 15 minutes are not counted.

Top 3 for Kauaʻi – Wildfire and all-hazard tests, ranked by people affected (at most two from one test). Full results: the Kauaʻi stress-test annex (a separate PDF, also linked in the app).

  1. Stranded visitors for 3 or 7 days – Kauaʻi
    28,577 visitors on an average day (island level).
  2. All four counties at once (wildfire)
    22,115 people who may need a public shelter (likely), counties together (cots: 22,116; shelter staff per shift: 1,534).
  3. Kauaʻi and Honolulu at once (wildfire)
    16,795 people who may need a public shelter (likely), counties together (cots: 16,796; shelter staff per shift: 1,163).

Sources, evidence and team assumptions for each result: in the annex.

Questions for counties

These need county knowledge. The plan has no data for them, so it shows no numbers.

  1. Shelter buildings in an earthquake or a strong hurricane: Which shelter buildings have been assessed for earthquake and hurricane-wind resilience, and which need a check before they are used after a strong event?
  2. Resupply for Molokaʻi and Lānaʻi: How would water, food, cots, fuel and medical supplies reach Molokaʻi and Lānaʻi if the harbours or airports close for several days, and who decides the order of shipments?
  3. Cell network outages: Which areas lose mobile phone service first in a long power outage or after wind damage, and how will warnings and shelter information reach them (radio, sirens, door-to-door)?
  4. Schools used as shelters: When a school is used as a shelter, how long can it stay open before classes must restart, and where do residents go next?
  5. Partner capacity: Could each county collect partner capacity (cots, meals, staff, vehicles, interpreters) with a county-run form such as Survey123, with no personal data, so the plan can show pledged capacity instead of 'confirm with partner'?
